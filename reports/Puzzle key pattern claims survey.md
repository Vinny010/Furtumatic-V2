# Every Claimed Puzzle-Key Pattern Fails Testing

Nothing claimed, tested or published about exploitable structure in the Bitcoin Puzzle keys, in secp256k1 coordinates, in hash160 addresses, or in non-generic ECDLP attacks has survived contact with the data or the literature. All **83 public puzzle keys** (#1–#70, #75, #80 … #130, #135) were recovered by exhaustive brute force or Pollard kangaroo on creator-exposed public keys; **zero solves are attributed to a pattern, filter or AI prior** ([itsnex1s/crypto-puzzle-list](https://github.com/itsnex1s/crypto-puzzle-list/blob/main/solved/bitcoin-puzzle-transaction-solved.md)). Recomputed against the current solved set, the keys' positions within their ranges are indistinguishable from uniform draws (**chi-square 8.72 on 9 df, KS p ≈ 0.76**), every "hot zone" theory has already been broken by later solves (#69 at 0.72 %, #66 at 25.6 %), and every attempt to recover the creator's "deterministic wallet" seed — 2^32 Mersenne Twister seeds, 2^32 glibc seeds, Java LCG algebra, Z3 state recovery, millions of BIP32 passphrases — returned nothing ([mlartab](https://github.com/mlartab/bitcoin-puzzle-systematic-analysis)). On the curve side, the only documented non-uniformity in secp256k1 public keys is that roughly half of field elements are valid x-coordinates, a property of the curve independent of the private key ([Elligator, ePrint 2013/325](https://eprint.iacr.org/2013/325)); bit-security theorems show that predicting even one coordinate bit with non-negligible advantage is equivalent to solving ECDH/ECDLP outright ([Boneh–Shparlinski 2001](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-44647-8_12)), and a 2023 theorem shows gradient-trained networks cannot learn even the parity bit of a discrete log ([Takhanov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01611)). Every structural ECDLP attack (MOV, anomalous, Weil descent, index calculus) needs a curve property secp256k1 was chosen not to have; the only structural gain that applies is a constant √6 fold of Pollard rho, and the best full prime-field ECDLP record stands at **114 bits** ([ellipticnews](https://ellipticnews.wordpress.com/2018/04/22/114-bit-ecdlp-solved-on-a-curve-with-automorphisms-over-a-prime-field/)). The one caveat that matters: most primary forum and paper hosts (bitcointalk, Reddit, arXiv, ePrint, SafeCurves) were unreachable from the research environment, so a number of findings rest on search snippets and GitHub mirrors rather than full reads; they are flagged below where it matters.

## The creator's "deterministic wallet" remark has been mined to exhaustion

The entire pattern-hunting genre descends from one sentence. On 2017-04-27 the puzzle's creator (handle saatoshi_rising, bitcointalk topic 1306983, message 18765941) wrote: **"There is no pattern. It is just consecutive keys from a deterministic wallet (masked with leading 000...0001 to set difficulty). It is simply a crude measuring instrument, of the cracking strength of the community"** ([agntn/puzzles](https://github.com/agntn/puzzles/blob/main/docs/app/utils/landing.ts); [HomelessPhD/BTC32](https://github.com/HomelessPhD/BTC32)). The community's reading of the mask is that puzzle n's key equals 2^(n−1) plus the low n−1 bits of the wallet's n-th key, so the top bit is forced to 1 and everything below it is whatever the wallet produced ([mlartab](https://github.com/mlartab/bitcoin-puzzle-systematic-analysis)). Readers have treated "deterministic wallet" as a hint that the seed, and therefore every unsolved key, is recoverable from the solved ones. The creator's later actions are the only "structure" that ever demonstrably helped: in July 2017 the funds from #161–#256 were folded into #1–#160, and on 2019-05-31/06-01 a 1,000-satoshi output was spent from every fifth address (#65, #70, #75 … #160), exposing those public keys and converting those lots from a 2^n address search into a 2^(n/2) interval discrete-log problem for Pollard kangaroo ([roadhero/Bitcoin-Puzzle-Info](https://github.com/roadhero/Bitcoin-Puzzle-Info)).

The seed-recovery hypothesis has been tested more thoroughly than any other claim in this survey, and every test came back negative. The most systematic effort (mlartab's "bitcoin-puzzle-systematic-analysis") enumerated **all 2^32 seeds of Python's Mersenne Twister** ("No seed worked"), **all 2^32 glibc rand() seeds** ("Nothing"), recovered Java's LCG state algebraically ("The puzzle keys don't match Java's LCG pattern"), attacked MT19937 state with Z3 and the `untwister` tool ("The SAT solver couldn't crack it"), ran millions of BIP32 passphrases across four derivation paths using rockyou.txt, Bitcoin vocabulary and creator-associated phrases ("The passphrase is not in any public wordlist"), and tried pre-BIP32 SHA-256 brainwallets with several separators ("Nothing"). The author's summary is "I didn't find the key to puzzle #71," and his structural conclusion is the important one: if the wallet is BIP32-like, "because SHA-512 is a one-way function, you cannot … compute child keys without the chain code," and "the chain code is 256 random bits. Brute-forcing it would take longer than the age of the universe" ([mlartab README](https://github.com/mlartab/bitcoin-puzzle-systematic-analysis)). A second project (lutfiEmre's "puzzle-71-research") fingerprinted the wallet from on-chain evidence — all 82 then-solved outputs came from compressed public keys, and the 2017 redistribution transaction had 96 compressed inputs and 1 uncompressed — and tested the Casascius-style SHA256(i/passphrase) formula, before concluding that this "does not prove BIP32 or any named wallet," that "with current public evidence there is no verifiable, computationally justified solution path," and closing the repository as `CLOSED_PENDING_NEW_PRIMARY_EVIDENCE` ([PUBKEY_FINGERPRINT.md](https://github.com/lutfiEmre/bitcoin-puzzle-71-research/blob/main/PUBKEY_FINGERPRINT.md); [README](https://github.com/lutfiEmre/bitcoin-puzzle-71-research)). A third (graffitilogic/KeyScanner) generated **a billion keys on 2015-era hardware and operating systems** hunting for OpenSSL `bn_rand_range` grouping anomalies and reported, "I don't think there is anything here" ([KeyScanner](https://github.com/graffitilogic/KeyScanner)).

The "consecutive keys" phrase also invites a naive test: do adjacent puzzle keys relate arithmetically? They do not. Across #5–#69 the ratio k(n+1)/k(n) ranges from 1.10 to 3.37 with mean 2.07 — exactly what independent uniform draws in doubling ranges produce — and the lag-1 autocorrelation of normalised positions is 0.066, noise level (computed from [BTC32_solved_unsolved.txt](https://github.com/HomelessPhD/BTC32/blob/main/BTC32_solved_unsolved.txt)). This is what the mask guarantees: "consecutive" describes wallet indices, not an additive relation between the published integers, so the published low bits behave as independent samples from the wallet's hash chain. The one context in which a consecutive-key relation is genuinely exploitable is unrelated to the puzzle: Bitcoin Core's own notes on descriptor wallets warn that with unhardened BIP32 derivation "leaking one derived private key is sufficient for an attacker to compute every private key," because the child-key tweak is derivable from the public xpub ([bitcoin/bitcoin PR 25366](https://github.com/bitcoin/bitcoin/pull/25366)). That attack needs the xpub; the puzzle publishes none. The fair reading of the creator's post is "I did not hand-pick the numbers," not "here is a hint." The original post and any follow-ups could not be read directly (bitcointalk was blocked), and no source names the wallet software, so the BIP32 and Casascius hypotheses remain untested guesses rather than refuted ones.

## Eighty-three solved keys sit where uniform draws would put them

Three position-in-range claims circulate. The most-cited is HomelessPhD/BTC32's 2021–2023 histogram analysis, which normalised each key to α = (k − 2^(n−1))/2^(n−1) and observed that "Private Keys are more probable to be found at {0.3-0.5} and {0.6-0.8}" with a "special case … {0.82-0.83} where few Private Keys lied closely." The second is a tracker statistic — "79 keys solved, average found at 53.5 %, 41 keys (51.9 %) found above the 50 % mark" — that readers turn into an "upper half" or "60–70 % band" strategy ([secretscan.org](https://secretscan.org/Bitcoin_puzzle), snippet only). The third is a folk heuristic that keys tend toward the centre and the edges are never hit. What is striking is that the author of the first claim never endorsed it: he offered three scenarios, one being that "each Private Key is generated using a strong (good) Random Generator and so all of them are uniformly distributed - the histogram has its peaks due to small 'observations number'," reported that "there is no any linear tendency or relation in data … Autocorrelation looks just like a noise autocorrelation," ran a MATLAB simulation of the same generation scheme showing that "the histogram for the first 60s numbers doesn't linked with the rest 100s histogram," and warned that a fitted polynomial "will ideally fit the dataset of n points … just memorizing the data instead of retrieving the logic behind the data" ([HomelessPhD/BTC32 README](https://github.com/HomelessPhD/BTC32)).

Recomputing against all 83 keys settles it. For n ≥ 5 (79 keys; #1–#4 have ranges too small for α to mean anything), the **mean position is 0.514, the median 0.502, and 50.6 % lie above the midpoint**. Decile counts are [5, 7, 7, 10, 10, 5, 14, 8, 6, 7] against 7.9 expected, giving **chi-square 8.72 on 9 degrees of freedom (p ≈ 0.46)**; the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic is **D = 0.075 (p ≈ 0.76)**; lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.066 (computed from [BTC32_solved_unsolved.txt](https://github.com/HomelessPhD/BTC32/blob/main/BTC32_solved_unsolved.txt)). The tracker's 53.5 % average is within one standard error of 50 % (the standard deviation of the mean of 79 uniforms is about 0.033) and cannot support any directional strategy. The "centre" claim fails outright: the 40–50 % decile holds 10 keys, the 50–60 % decile holds 5. The one visually striking feature, five keys within the 1 %-wide bin 82–83 % (#9, #40, #51, #59, #80) against 0.8 expected, is a post-hoc selection among roughly 100 such bins and is exactly the kind of artefact HomelessPhD's simulation warned about. The time-ordered test is the decisive one: of the four address-only puzzles solved after the band theory was published, #67 (79.8 %) and #68 (49.0 %) fell inside the 30–50/60–80 bands and **#66 (25.6 %) and #69 (0.72 %) fell outside** — two hits out of four for bands covering about 40 % of the range, which is chance. Any "skip the edges" rule would have missed #10 (0.39 %), #69 (0.72 %), #63 (95.0 %), #31 (95.8 %), #60 (96.9 %) and #25 (97.8 %). Trackers themselves attribute #69's quick fall to its position "at the very beginning of the search range (0.72 %)" ([privatekeys.pw](https://privatekeys.pw/puzzles/bitcoin-puzzle-tx), snippet only; [itsnex1s](https://github.com/itsnex1s/crypto-puzzle-list/blob/main/solved/bitcoin-puzzle-transaction-solved.md)). Two convention traps explain some circulating figures: some sites measure position over [0, 2^n) rather than [2^(n−1), 2^n), which pushes every value upward (#66 becomes 62.8 %, #69 becomes 50.4 %), and #70's key 0x349b84b6431a6c4ef1 sits at 64.4 % of its range, not the 40 % sometimes quoted (the nearest key to 40 % is #90 at 40.23 %).

The other "patterns" people report are artefacts of the mask. Leading hex digits for n ≥ 5 are heavily skewed toward 1, 2 and 3 ({1:20, 2:11, 3:9, 4:5, …}), but this is forced: when (n−1) mod 4 = 0 the leading digit must be 1, when it equals 1 the digit must be 2 or 3, and so on. Below the forced bit, the fraction of 1-bits among the free bits (n ≥ 20) ranges 0.348–0.667 with mean 0.495, consistent with fair coin flips (computed from the same file). With 83 samples these tests have little power against subtle biases, but they rule out the strong claims — a 20–40 %-wide band holding most keys, or empty edges — that would be needed to speed a search materially. No public source applies a chi-square or KS test to the solved positions; HomelessPhD's is the only write-up with any formal statistics, and it ends without claiming a pattern. Reputed sceptics on bitcointalk (kTimesG, WanderingPhilospher, nomachine appear in snippets) could not be read, so the originator of the "60–70 % band" phrasing and its rebuttals remain unidentified.

### Non-uniform scan orders have no documented solve, and plain methods took everything

A cottage industry of tools promises to reorder or filter the keyspace. OlivierGaland's pool client tries "to select only range that are statistically more likely to be the solution" with filters that discard ranges "repeating to many times same char like for instance 0xAAAAA789," enforce bit balance between 30 % and 70 %, and match bit patterns; its README states "no empirical results are provided" ([bitcoin-puzzle-worker](https://github.com/OlivierGaland/bitcoin-puzzle-worker)). allinbit/btc_puzzle claims that constraining 0/1 counts "will reduce the key space to about 44.56 % of the puzzle 66," offers no derivation, and tells users to "try your luck" ([btc_puzzle](https://github.com/allinbit/btc_puzzle)). binqbit's bit-scan v2 uses "an intentionally non-uniform, analytics-guided bit-pattern generator" it never describes ([bit-scan](https://github.com/binqbit/bit-scan)). accessor-io/pattern tried to derive #68 from #67's key via "mathematical patterns," bitwise operations and a "RowHammer-inspired" bit-flip search, generated 321,270 candidates and "has not yet found a match" ([pattern](https://github.com/accessor-io/pattern)); #68 was subsequently found by brute force at 49.0 % of its range. ACD421's "bitcoin-puzzle-solvers" advertises a "TensorFlow model trained to predict high-fitness regions" for #135 with "no reported results, performance metrics, or success claims" ([ACD421](https://github.com/ACD421/bitcoin-puzzle-solvers)); a "torus topology" kangaroo for #135 likewise reports "no actual solved puzzles or empirical performance data" ([cassietorusbtc135solver](https://github.com/sjgant80-hub/cassietorusbtc135solver)). #135 then fell to RetiredCoder's plain RCKangaroo. Address-prefix approaches — a YouTube channel publishing #71-range keys whose address matches the first eight characters of the target, and a bitcointalk thread "THE PREFIX DILEMMA" opened 2025-11-07 — are near-miss detectors, not priors; nothing found claims prefix matching locates the key ([BitcoinPuzzle71Prefixes](https://www.youtube.com/@BitcoinPuzzle71Prefixes); [topic 5564718](https://bitcointalk.org/index.php?topic=5564718.0), title only). Because the solved keys' free-bit balance spans 0.35–0.67 and their positions are flat, entropy and pattern filters can only discard real probability mass; #69's key 0x101d83275fb2bc7e0c is exactly the low-entropy, near-boundary value such filters throw away.

What actually solved the puzzles is mundane. The Large Bitcoin Collider swept sequentially from the bottom; its log shows 51-bit completion on 2017-04-09 and 52-bit on 2017-04-23, matching the #51/#52 solve dates ([itsnex1s](https://github.com/itsnex1s/crypto-puzzle-list/blob/main/solved/bitcoin-puzzle-transaction-solved.md)). #66 (2024-09-12), #67 (2025-02-21), #68 (2025-04-06) and #69 (2025-04-30) fell to GPU brute force with BitCrack/KeyHunt-class tools; #66's key was extracted from the mempool and the prize front-run at 406 sat/vB, #67 and #68 were submitted out-of-band to avoid that fate, and #69's winner paid 2,515 sat/vB in a replacement auction ([roadhero](https://github.com/roadhero/Bitcoin-Puzzle-Info); [btc-hunter-julia](https://github.com/SamuelOliveiraBRA/btc-hunter-julia); [bitcoin/bitcoin#29793](https://github.com/bitcoin/bitcoin/issues/29793)). One tracker labels #67/#68 "Kangaroo on exposed public key," but no public key for either was exposed before the solver's own spend; the roadhero and news accounts describing GPU brute force should be preferred. The exposed-pubkey lots fell to kangaroo: JeanLucPons solved #110 "in 2.1 days (109 bit key) using 256 Tesla V100 … 2^55.55 group operations," and RetiredCoder solved **#135 on 2026-07-28 after about five months on ~200 RTX 4090/5090 GPUs, finishing at roughly one-third of expected work** — luck within kangaroo's variance, not a prior — posting "I quit, officially" and releasing RCKangaroo v4.0 95 minutes later ([itsnex1s](https://github.com/itsnex1s/crypto-puzzle-list/blob/main/solved/bitcoin-puzzle-transaction-solved.md); [agntn/puzzles](https://github.com/agntn/puzzles/blob/main/docs/app/utils/landing.ts)). The stark pattern, as one tracker puts it, is that "no address-only lot above #70 has fallen in 11 years"; the btcpuzzle.info #71 pool had scanned 0.864 % of its 33,554,432 sub-ranges at 57.3 Gkeys/s as of June 2026, with a projected 422 years to completion ([news.bitcoin.com](https://news.bitcoin.com/key-hunters-eye-58-87m-bitcoin-puzzle-as-916-btc-sits-unsolved-in-78-addresses/), snippet only). Note that some 2025–2026 trackers still list "82 solved" or "#135 unsolved"; the 83-key figure follows the HomelessPhD file updated 2026-07-30.

## Public keys and addresses carry no information about the scalar

The one real, documented statistical non-uniformity in secp256k1 public keys is old and useless for key recovery. Only about half of all field elements are valid x-coordinates (the Legendre symbol of x³+7 decides), so raw curve points "are easily distinguishable from uniform random strings of bits" — which is the motivation for Elligator ([ePrint 2013/325](https://eprint.iacr.org/2013/325)) and, for prime-order curves such as secp256k1 that lack the order-2 point Elligator needs, for Elligator Squared ([ePrint 2014/043](https://eprint.iacr.org/2014/043)). Langley's implementation note spells out the distinguisher: a valid point's x satisfies the test always, "while it only holds for a random number in about 50 % of the cases" ([ImperialViolet](https://www.imperialviolet.org/2013/12/25/elligator.html), snippet). SafeCurves records secp256k1 as failing its "ind" criterion for exactly this reason ([SafeCurves](https://safecurves.cr.yp.to/), snippet). But this is a censorship-resistance concern, not a leak: it tells an observer that a string is a curve point, which for a Bitcoin public key is already known. Within the set of valid points, k → kG is a bijection from Z_n onto the group, so for uniform k the point is exactly uniform and no statistic of (x, y) can depend on which k produced it; character-sum results of Shparlinski's school make the equidistribution of coordinates over intervals of k precise, with error terms of order √p, i.e. relative deviations around 2^−128 ([Shparlinski, J. Number Theory 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022314X14000547), snippet).

The formal literature goes further than "no dependence is known." Boneh and Shparlinski proved at CRYPTO 2001 that "just predicting one bit of the Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman secret in a family of curves is as hard as computing the entire secret" ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-44647-8_12), snippet); Jetchev and Venkatesan extended this from the least significant bit to any bit ([CRYPTO 2008](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1007/978-3-540-85174-5_5)); Duc and Jetchev showed that "if one can predict any of the bits of the input to an elliptic curve based one-way function over a finite field, then we can invert the function" ([ePrint 2011/329](https://eprint.iacr.org/2011/329.pdf)); Shani refined the ECDH results at PKC 2017 ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-662-54365-8_15)). A detectable correlation between any coordinate bit and k would therefore not be a "pattern" — it would be a solver for the same problem Bitcoin's security already assumes is hard (the reductions run over a family of related curves rather than one fixed curve, a technicality that does not weaken the practical conclusion). Related keys give related points only through the group law: (k+c)G = kG + cG, (2k)G = 2(kG), and on this j = 0 curve (λk)G = (βx, y) for the cube root of unity λ ([aburan28/crypto PR #483](https://github.com/aburan28/crypto/pull/483), fetched). These relations are public, are already fully consumed by rho/kangaroo symmetry folding, and yield nothing about k unless c is known and one key is already known.

Hash160 can only subtract information. SHA-256 followed by RIPEMD-160 compresses 256-bit keys to 160 bits, so roughly 2^96 private keys share each address; generic preimage cost is 2^160, against ~2^128 for ECDLP, and Grover only cuts the hash to about 80 bits and "reachable only if an address is reused" ([Quantum Horizon, arXiv 2606.14484](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.14484), snippet). The best published cryptanalysis of RIPEMD-160 reaches collisions on **40 of 80 steps** (EUROCRYPT 2023), with no full-round preimage attack for either hash ([ePrint 2023/285](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/285)). Hobbyist address scanners that flag "very few unique bytes, repeated single bytes" ([Bitcoin-Address-Analyzer](https://github.com/ethicbrudhack/-Bitcoin-Address-Analyzer)) and a Medium analysis of RIPEMD-160 digit runs find addresses consistent with randomness ([Finlow-Bates](https://medium.com/@keir_85660/looking-at-ripemd-160-bitcoin-addresses-for-fun-and-no-profit-15f311521fce), snippet). No peer-reviewed study runs NIST SP 800-22, Dieharder or TestU01 on streams of secp256k1 coordinates or addresses — a genuine gap, almost certainly because the outcome is theoretically determined (raw compressed keys would fail on the 0x02/0x03 prefix and the QR filter; hash160 outputs would pass) and bears on nothing. The one hobbyist "distinguisher" result, a GRU reaching "approximately 87.83 % validation accuracy" separating curve points from random strings in a 1:8 mix, is explained by the base rate of the majority class (88.9 %) plus the known half-of-x structure, and its own README "does not claim actual private key recovery" ([demining](https://github.com/demining/An-attempt-to-predict-the-behavior-of-the-properties-of-the-secp256k1-Elliptic-Curve), fetched). The only academic recoveries of keys from addresses — Sala, Sogiorno and Taufer's 2020 "small subgroup attack" and Di Scala et al.'s 2022 extension across Ethereum, Dogecoin, Litecoin, Dash, Zcash and Bitcoin Cash — enumerate tiny structured subsets of k and hash forward against every address ever seen ([MDPI 2020](https://mdpi.com/2227-7390/8/10/1645/htm); [arXiv 2206.14107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14107)). They exploit bad key generation and are, if anything, evidence for the one-way model: the only way anyone has inverted address → key is to guess k from a small candidate set.

### Machine learning has never beaten a coin flip on a key bit

The strongest formal attempt is a negative result dressed as a positive title. "Mage: Cracking Elliptic Curve Cryptography with Cross-Axis Transformers" (arXiv, December 2025) trained a **784M-parameter** byte-level transformer on 100,000 (public key, private key) pairs and evaluated on 38,200; the model "achieved 99 % accuracy on the training set, but as training loss decreased, evaluation loss increased, indicating overfitting," a second 350-epoch run on fresh random pairs "saw zero improvement in the model, with loss staying flatline," and the author concedes it "would make a far better Rainbow Table than an Elliptic Curve simulation" ([arXiv 2512.12483](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12483), snippets). The paper's extrapolation to a 405B-parameter model that could "compromise half of all private keys" is speculation unsupported by its own held-out numbers, and secondary coverage headlined it as enabling cracking regardless ([Quantum Zeitgeist](https://quantumzeitgeist.com/security-mage-enables-cracking-elliptic-curve-cryptography-risks-modern/)). The curve size Mage used could not be verified because arXiv was blocked. Every GitHub "predictor" repository examined — an MLP mapping public key to private key ([ufodia](https://github.com/ufodia/Bitcoin-Private-Key-Predictor)), a transformer from address string to key that calls itself a "poof of concept" ([sayajinpt](https://github.com/sayajinpt/Bitcoin-Private-Key-Predictor-Tranformer)), an LSTM "toy example" with 37 stars and two commits ([btc-room101](https://github.com/btc-room101/bitcoin-rnn)), a conceptual README that concedes the task is "extremely complex" ([DRSZL](https://github.com/DRSZL/BitcoinTensorFlowPrivateKeyPrediction)), and a 2021 bitcointalk RNN thread ([topic 5337128](https://bitcointalksearch.org/topic/neural-networks-and-secp256k1-5337128/all.html), snippet) — reports no held-out accuracy at all, which is the signature of a negative result the author chose not to write down. HomelessPhD explicitly declined to fit neural networks to the puzzle keys, noting that "a model you are using for prognosis should have a number of parameters less than a number of observations" ([BTC32](https://github.com/HomelessPhD/BTC32)).

Theory explains the silence. Takhanov et al. (ACML 2023) proved that for the parity bit of a discrete logarithm in a prime-order group, "the gradient of the loss function concentrates around a fixed point, independent of the logarithm's base," giving "a restricted ability to learn the parity bit efficiently using gradient-based methods, irrespective of the complexity of the network architecture," with experiments showing success rates falling toward chance as the group grows ([PMLR v222](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v222/takhanov24a.html); [arXiv 2310.01611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01611)); a companion paper extends the argument to modular-arithmetic function classes ([arXiv 2310.12660](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12660)). A positive per-bit result would thus contradict a published theorem for gradient methods, and combined with the bit-security results above, any learner that generalised would be an ECDLP solver. The credible dissent is scoped and instructive: Gohr's neural distinguishers beat classical differential tables on reduced-round Speck32/64 but decay from 92.9 % at 5 rounds to **51.4 % at 8 of 22 rounds** ([ePrint 2019/037](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/037.pdf)); "fuzzy bits" networks invert only a few rounds of MD5/SHA-1/SHA-256 ([arXiv 1901.02438](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.02438)); and SALSA's transformers recover LWE secrets only for dimension ≤ 128 and Hamming weight ≤ 4 ([ePrint 2022/935](https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/935)). Learning wins where a primitive has exploitable structure — few rounds, sparse secrets, a noise channel — and full-round SHA-256, RIPEMD-160 and secp256k1 scalar multiplication have none. The cryptographers' framing is the same argument from the other side: "Machine learning works by exploiting redundancy … done right, ciphertext has no such redundancy … so the model has nothing to grip" ([postquantum.com](https://postquantum.com/ai-security/ai-break-encryption/), snippet). The 2025–2026 "AI cracked Bitcoin" headlines dissolve on inspection: the July 2025 movement of 80,000 BTC from eight 14-year-dormant wallets was judged owner-initiated, with Polygon's Mihailo Bjelic calling the AI theory "fake news" ([CryptoTimes](https://www.cryptotimes.io/2025/07/06/ai-tools-have-cracked-bitcoin-private-keys-fact-check/), snippet), and the May 2026 "Claude cracked a wallet" story was AI-assisted recovery of the owner's own backup file and password, "meaning no bitcoin cryptography was broken" ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/05/14/claude-helps-recover-usd395-000-in-bitcoin-trapped-on-a-computer-for-years), snippet). The "key finder" ecosystem is economically rational mainly as malware delivery — Kaspersky's GitVenom campaign stole roughly $456K–$485K in BTC via fake GitHub projects with polished, often AI-generated READMEs ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/02/gitvenom-malware-steals-456k-in-bitcoin.html), snippet) — and several open tools state the intent outright ("steal bitcoins by checking private keys for balance," [shlima/fortune](https://github.com/shlima/fortune)).

## Structural ECDLP attacks stall at a √6 constant factor

Every sub-exponential or polynomial ECDLP algorithm in the literature requires a curve property secp256k1 was selected not to have. The MOV/Frey–Rück pairing transfer needs a small embedding degree; secp256k1's is about **1.9 × 10^76** ([std.neuromancer.sk](https://std.neuromancer.sk/secg/secp256k1/), snippet; [Elementary Bitcoin](https://elementarybitcoin.org/chapters/05-secp256k1.html)). The Smart/Satoh–Araki/Semaev anomalous attack needs trace 1 (#E = p); secp256k1 is listed "Anomalous: false" (same sources). Weil descent, GHS and every practical summation-polynomial index-calculus variant need an extension field; secp256k1 is defined over a prime field, and a 2026 community survey states flatly that "all index calculus and descent attacks require extension fields for practical prime-field curves," that the "first-fall-degree assumption" behind binary-field complexity claims is "now widely doubted," and that prime-field index calculus is "open-but-unmoved" ([aburan28/crypto PR #19](https://github.com/aburan28/crypto/pull/19), fetched). The primary results agree: Semaev's 2015 sub-exponential claim was for binary fields ([ePrint 2015/310](https://eprint.iacr.org/2015/310)); Galbraith and Gebregiyorgis found "Pollard rho is still much faster than index calculus algorithms for the ECDLP over prime extension fields of reasonable size" ([ePrint 2014/806](https://eprint.iacr.org/2014/806)); Petit, Kosters and Messeng's prime-field approach works only "when p − 1 has a smooth factor" ([PKC 2016](https://christophe.petit.web.ulb.be/files/16PKC_primeECDLP.pdf)); a complexity-bounds paper concludes Semaev's naive index calculus "cannot be more efficient than the rho method over an arbitrary finite field" and empirically "cannot be more efficient than even the brute force method" ([J. Math. Cryptology](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jmc-2019-0029/html)); the largest summation polynomial ever computed is the sixth ([ePrint 2017/609](https://eprint.iacr.org/2017/609.pdf)); and a 2024 implementation study confirms the prime-field variants are "only feasible for small parameters" ([ePrint 2024/1923](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1923)). Shoup's generic-group lower bound of Ω(√p) group operations is what all of this leaves standing ([Shoup 1997](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-69053-0_18)).

The single structural feature that does apply is the j = 0 endomorphism, and its effect is exactly quantified: the rho walk can be folded by all six automorphisms for a **√6 speed-up** versus √2 from the negation map alone, measured at 2.69× (theory 2.45×) on a toy 36-bit curve ([aburan28/crypto PR #483](https://github.com/aburan28/crypto/pull/483), fetched), leaving roughly 2^127 expected operations. The remaining attack classes that touch secp256k1 in practice are not ECDLP results. Twist attacks recover a key modulo small factors of the twist order (3319, 22639, 109903, …) and combine by CRT, but only against an ECDH-style implementation that multiplies an attacker-supplied point without validation; the attack "does not compromise ECDSA signature verification" and is defeated by on-curve checks or compressed keys, which Bitcoin's library performs ([mathengem/twist](https://github.com/mathengem/twist/blob/master/2020_05_26_secp256k1_twist_attacks/secp256k1_twist_attacks.md), fetched; the twist cofactor is given as 3·13²·… there and 3²·13²·… in SafeCurves-derived snippets, an unresolved discrepancy). Lattice and hidden-number-problem attacks on ECDSA need leaked nonce bits: Breitner and Heninger recovered "hundreds of Bitcoin private keys" from biased nonces, worth about $54 in total ([ePrint 2019/023](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/023)), and LadderLeak broke ECDSA with under one bit of nonce leakage against a specific OpenSSL timing flaw ([ePrint 2020/615](https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/615.pdf)). These are the only attack classes with real Bitcoin losses on record, and both are implementation failures.

The computational record confirms the theory. The best full prime-field ECDLP solved is **114 bits** (a Barreto–Naehrig curve with the same order-6 automorphisms, ~2,000 CPU cores for ~6 months, 2018) ([ellipticnews](https://ellipticnews.wordpress.com/2018/04/22/114-bit-ecdlp-solved-on-a-curve-with-automorphisms-over-a-prime-field/)), ahead of the 2009 generic 112-bit record on more than 200 PlayStation 3s over 62.6 PS3-years ([Bos et al.](https://www.joppebos.com/files/rho_ps3.pdf)) and the 117.35-bit binary-field FPGA record ([ePrint 2016/382](https://eprint.iacr.org/2016/382)); a 2025 survey places classical prime-field records "near 112–113 bits," apparently excluding the special BN curve ([arXiv 2508.14011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.14011), snippet). The puzzle's kangaroo solves (#130 at ~2^65 operations, #135 at ~2^67) are larger in nominal bits but are interval problems, not full ECDLP. Either way the distance to a 256-bit instance is 2^60–2^70, and prime-field records have moved two bits since 2009 despite a decade of GPU growth — the pace of constant-factor engineering, not algorithmic change. The only sustained new-algorithm line in 2024–2026 is Mahalanobis's "zero minor" reduction of ECDLP to linear algebra, verified "for groups of order up to 2^50" ([arXiv 2310.04132](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04132); [Experimental Mathematics 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10586458.2025.2525844)), whose July 2026 "Guess and Determine" sequel claims polynomial time for the determine step only, states no overall complexity, and notes the attack "poses no security risk to elliptic curve cryptography at this time" ([arXiv 2607.09814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09814), snippet). An automated 2026 review of 131 prime-field ECDLP proposals found "no sub-exponential ECDLP breakthroughs," with one headline result "mathematically void" and another refuted by a counterexample at p = 13 ([crypto-autoresearcher PR #719](https://github.com/aburan28/crypto-autoresearcher/pull/719), fetched); Epoch AI's FrontierMath ECDLP open problem, built so that a fast solve "would suggest that the AI found a new attack," reports none ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/frontiermath/open-problems/elliptic-curve-discrete-logarithm)). Where LLM agents have moved the needle is on the quantum side — the ECDSA.Fail contest cut a reversible secp256k1 point-addition circuit's cost by 86 % ([arXiv 2609.09582](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09582)) — and there the 2026 resource estimates have dropped to ≤1,200 logical qubits and <500,000 physical qubits in Google's superconducting model ([arXiv 2603.28846](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.28846)), 835 logical qubits in Luo et al. ([arXiv 2607.13816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13816)), and a fully compiled 19,397-physical-qubit trapped-ion blueprint at 25.7 days per attempt from IonQ ([ePrint 2026/1916](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1916)), while the largest ECDLP run on real quantum hardware is **15 bits** ([Quantum Insider](https://thequantuminsider.com/2026/04/24/project-eleven-q-day-prize-quantum-ecc-attack/)) and a June 2026 forecast puts a cryptographically relevant machine at one-in-six by 2035 ([arXiv 2606.14484](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14484)). Shor is the only credible non-generic route to a secp256k1 key, and it is not a statistical or structural attack in the sense the puzzle community means.

## Conclusion

The survey's most useful result is not that the claims are wrong but how they are wrong in the same way. Each one either mistakes a property of the puzzle's construction (the forced top bit, the creator's choice of which public keys to expose, the ~50 % of field elements that are valid x-coordinates) for a property of the private keys, or reads a small-sample fluctuation as a signal and then fails to survive the next solve. The only "structure" that has ever shortened a solve is the creator's own pubkey reveal, which halves the exponent for every fifth puzzle, plus the luck of where individual keys happened to sit — and even that luck is symmetric, since #69's 0.72 % is matched by #25's 97.8 %. The bit-security and learning-hardness theorems close the loop: a pattern that worked would be a solver, and a solver would be the discovery of the decade, not a GitHub README with no results section.

For anyone deciding where to spend compute, the implication is blunt. For address-only puzzles the expected cost of any scan order is the same as any other, and every filter that discards keyspace lowers the probability of success by exactly the fraction it discards; for exposed-pubkey puzzles kangaroo's √(πn/2)/√6-style cost is the floor and its variance is the only source of "early" wins. The frontier that is actually moving is quantum resource estimation — from ~2,300 logical qubits in 2017 to 835 in July 2026 — and its practical target population is precisely the keys whose public key is already on-chain, the same lots the kangaroo community has been draining classically. The honest gaps that remain are evidentiary, not scientific: the original bitcointalk threads, the Mage full text, and the 2026 ePrint listings could not be read here, so an obscure but flawed preprint or a forum rebuttal could have been missed; nothing found suggests any of them would change the picture.
